Boards have been asked to plan for an unusual amount of uncertainty in recent years: interest rates, trade policy, energy prices, the pace of AI adoption. Many have responded by commissioning scenario work. Few would say it changed what they decided.

The usual pattern is familiar. A strategy team or outside adviser develops four carefully described futures, presents them at an offsite, and the board discusses them thoughtfully. Then the scenarios are filed, the budget is set on a single central forecast, and the exercise is repeated two years later with new scenarios.

The problem is not the scenarios. It is that they are disconnected from decisions. Scenario planning becomes useful to a board when it answers a narrower question: what would we do differently, and when would we know we needed to?

## Fewer scenarios, sharper differences

Four or five richly described scenarios are hard to hold in mind and harder to act on. We generally recommend three, built around the two or three uncertainties that matter most to the company's economics, and defined by numbers rather than narratives.

For a manufacturer with heavy European operations, the critical uncertainties might be energy prices and demand in its largest end market. For a regional bank, it might be the path of interest rates and credit losses in commercial property. Each scenario sets a range for these variables and shows what happens to revenue, margin, cash and covenants. That is something a board can test.

## Agree the moves in advance

The most useful part of the exercise is deciding, in advance, what the company would do in each scenario. Some moves make sense in every case and should simply be made now. Others make sense only in one scenario and should be prepared but not triggered. A few are hedges that cost little and protect against the worst outcomes.

A simple way to set this out for a board:

- **No-regret moves:** actions that are right in all three scenarios, approved now
- **Prepared moves:** actions planned in detail, with budgets and owners, ready to launch if a trigger is reached
- **Hedges:** inexpensive options that limit damage in the downside scenario, such as flexible contracts or committed credit lines
- **Big bets:** actions that pay off only in one scenario, reserved for when the evidence is clear

## Define the triggers

A prepared move is only useful if the board knows when to launch it. That requires triggers: observable indicators that a scenario is unfolding, with thresholds set in advance. An energy price sustained above a defined level for a quarter. Order intake below a set percentage of plan for two consecutive months. A competitor announcing capacity in a key market.

> The value of a trigger is that it removes the debate about whether conditions have changed enough to act. That debate is where most of the delay comes from.

Management should report against the triggers at every board meeting, in a single page. Most of the time, nothing will have been triggered and the discussion will take five minutes. When something has, the board can act within weeks instead of quarters.

## Make it part of the calendar

Scenario planning works best when it is folded into existing routines rather than run as a separate project. The scenarios are refreshed once a year as part of the strategy cycle, the trigger dashboard is part of every board pack, and the budget includes the cost of prepared moves as contingent items.

This approach asks less of the board in terms of imagination and more in terms of discipline. In our experience, boards prefer it. They do not need to be persuaded that the future is uncertain. They need a way to act when it becomes clearer, without waiting for the next strategy offsite to decide.
